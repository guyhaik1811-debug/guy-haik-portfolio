We use the word "motivation" all the time, and most of us never stop to ask what it means. In my studies I read an article in Hebrew about motivation to learn in school (Kaplan and Assor, 2001, in the journal *Chinuch Hachshiva*) and answered ten questions about it. This article is what stayed with me, written again from the original research, with links so you can check it yourself.

It is an overview, not research of my own, and it is not advice about any particular student.

## Motivation is more than "how much"

When people say a student is "motivated" or "not motivated," they usually mean how hard the student works. The article I read, following the researcher Martin Maehr, describes motivation along three dimensions: **direction** (what a person chooses to do, and keeps doing when it gets hard), **intensity** (how much effort goes into it) and **quality** (how the person goes about it, for example solving a problem in a new way compared with working hard on a strategy that does not work). I have not checked Maehr's own papers, so treat this part as the article's description.

I like this because it explains something I had noticed: two students can work equally hard, and still be doing very different things.

## Idea 1: A reward can change why you do something

In 1973 Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett ran an experiment with preschool children who already liked to draw. They tested the idea that [a person's interest in an activity can drop when they are led to do it as a means to an outside reward](https://typeset.io/pdf/undermining-children-s-intrinsic-interest-with-extrinsic-3qn44yqiou.pdf). Children who were offered a "good player" award for drawing were [less likely to draw on their own afterwards](https://perlego.com/fr/book/1836371/intrinsic-and-extrinsic-motivation-the-search-for-optimal-motivation-and-performance-pdf), according to a later book summary of the study.

The honest version has two sides. This is one of the best-known findings in the field, but later work is mixed: one review concluded that [the effect is temporary and may not appear at all under some kinds of reward](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742036). So I would not read it as "never reward children." I read it as a reason to ask what a reward teaches a student about why they are doing the task.

## Idea 2: Three needs

Self-determination theory, from Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, says that [three innate psychological needs, competence, autonomy and relatedness, lead to better self-motivation and well-being when they are met, and to lower motivation when they are blocked](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/).

In plain words: people tend to stay engaged when they feel capable, when they have some say in what they do, and when they feel they belong.

This is where I ended up writing about myself in the assignment. In elementary school I did my homework because it was required, by the school or by my parents, and not because I wanted to learn. When I chose to study at university, it was mostly a choice of my own. The activity was similar. The reason was different, and the reason is what this theory is about.

## Idea 3: Which goal, and what you believe about ability

Researchers separate **mastery goals**, [learning goals about mastering new skills and understanding more](https://education.okstate.edu/site-files/documents/motivation-classrooms/motivation-minute-achievement-goal-orientations.pdf), from **performance goals**, which are about doing better than others or showing ability.

Carol Dweck and Ellen Leggett, in a 1988 paper, described [two patterns that the research kept finding: a "helpless" response and a "mastery-oriented" response](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295x.95.2.256). Dweck's earlier work describes the mastery pattern as [seeking challenge and persisting in the face of obstacles](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/1986-dweck.pdf). Their explanation links the patterns to a student's beliefs about intelligence, whether it is [something that can change or something fixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_theories_of_intelligence).

What I find useful is the question behind it. It is not only what students are trying to do in class, but why they are doing it.

## Idea 4: What a classroom can change

Ames, in a 1992 paper, used Epstein's framework to describe six features of a classroom that shape the goals students see around them. They are known as [TARGET](https://phaidra.univie.ac.at/detail/o:1323700): **T**ask (what the work is), **A**uthority (who decides), **R**ecognition (what gets praised), **G**rouping (how students are split into groups), **E**valuation (how learning is judged) and **T**ime (how the time is used).

A study in secondary schools in the Netherlands found that [two of these, task design and time, predicted students' mastery goals](https://core.ac.uk/download/286686864.pdf), though time was also linked to avoidance goals. One study is not a rule, but it shows the framework can be tested.

## What I take from it

I do not think these theories give a recipe. What they gave me is better questions to ask: why is this student doing the task, what would they do if no one were watching, and what does the way we reward and grade tell them about what matters?

The same questions work outside school, at work and in personal life. In a job, most people are probably moved by both an inner wish to grow and an outside reward like pay, and that is fine. It helps to know which one is doing the work.
