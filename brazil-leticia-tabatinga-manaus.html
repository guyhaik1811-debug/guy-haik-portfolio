Leticia, Tabatinga and Manaus, December 2017

December 2017Guy Haik

This chapter starts where the Colombia chapter ends. I flew to Leticia, the Colombian town on the Amazon, stayed one night, and walked across the border to Tabatinga, in Brazil. I left Tabatinga on December 22, 2017, on a slow river boat called M. Fernandes, slept three nights in a hammock on the deck, and reached Manaus on the night between December 25 and 26. I stayed in Manaus until about December 30.

My conclusion up front: do not buy the cheapest hammock, which is what I did. I would also take an app car instead of looking for a taxi at night, and I connected strongly with the Brazilian buffet where they weigh your plate. I do not remember what the boat ticket or the hammock cost, so the prices below come from sources I read in November 2026, each with its date.

About the times: the file names of my photos show my phone's clock, which stayed on Colombian time (GMT-5). Google Photos shows the local time in Brazil (GMT-4), one hour later, and I use that one. The boxes hold research I did in November 2026, each with a source and a date, so you can tell it apart from what I remember.

The way to Leticia, including the failed attempt to get there without flying, is in the Colombia chapter on Cali and San Cipriano.

Leticia, one night

I stayed one night in a hostel in Leticia. Because the boat left from Tabatinga on December 22 and I walked there the day after the night in Leticia, that night was December 21. I have no photos from Leticia and I do not remember the name of the hostel.

What surprised me was how touristy the town is. There were many agencies, and people come to Leticia to do a trek in the Amazon. I did not expect that. I also remember eating tapioca there, which I remember as a kind of rice omelette. The Brazilian dish with that name is, as far as I know, a crepe made from cassava starch, so what I remember as rice may have been that. I did not check what it was made of.

December 22: Tabatinga and the boat

The next day I walked for about half an hour to Tabatinga, a Brazilian town that runs straight into Leticia. In Tabatinga everything changed at once: they pay in reais and speak Portuguese. At a stall I asked, on the spur of the moment, when there was a boat to Manaus. The answer was: "Today. Buy a hammock and come."

I needed a hammock, so I stopped a man on a motorbike, again without planning it, and he took me to a place that sold them. I bought the cheapest one. I do not recommend that. I do not remember what it cost or what the ticket cost.

The boat was M. Fernandes, a three-deck white river boat tied to a muddy bank. I remember leaving around noon, but my photos say otherwise: the boat was still at the pier at 14:15 and at 15:31, and my video of the river as we left is from 17:46. I trust the photos.

The boat from Tabatinga to Manaus (checked November 2026)

What I found: a guide published on April 30, 2026 and last modified on September 27, 2026 says the slow boat from Tabatinga to Manaus cost 290 reais in hammock class in mid-April 2026, and took 78 hours for the author, about four days and three nights. It says slow boats do not run every day, and that delays and cancellations happen. The fast boat cost 1,010 reais, ran daily at 10:00 and took about 30 hours. You buy your own hammock in Tabatinga or Leticia (40 reais for the cheapest, with 10 more for rope), and tickets are sold at the river terminal in Tabatinga, which is not open around the clock. Colombian pesos are not accepted, and paying by card adds a 10% fee. Three meals a day were included, you bring your own plate, cup and cutlery, and filtered drinking water was free. The author describes the nights as cold and windy and says the lower deck was cooler. The boat docked in Manaus at about 19:00 (nomadicbackpacker.com). A guide published in April 2026 and updated on November 17, 2026 says the border post at Tabatinga is about 15 minutes on foot from the centre of Leticia, and that stalls in Tabatinga use reais and do not take cards (colombiamove.com).

What I did not find: an official timetable or price list from the boat companies or the port, anything on M. Fernandes in 2026, or a source for 2017. These are one blogger's figures from 2026, so treat them as a guide and confirm them in Tabatinga. I also did not check the visa rules for your passport. The same guide says to get your exit stamp at the immigration office at Leticia airport and your entry stamp at the Polícia Federal in Tabatinga within 24 hours. I do not remember doing either, so check this one carefully.

December 22 to 25: three nights on the deck

I slept on the hammock I bought in Tabatinga, on the hammock deck, under a ceiling covered in orange life jackets. Food was included in the ticket. The top deck had a small canteen where you could buy things like peanuts. I do not remember what else it sold, or the prices.

There was a small shower stall and a toilet that sends everything straight into the river. I think the water in the shower came from a tank, but I am not sure. It was clean enough, and "enough" is relative. There was no queue and no separation between men and women. The blogger in the box above writes that there were separate bathrooms for men and women on his boat in 2026. I do not remember it that way.

The river is wide and brown, and the banks are a low green line most of the time. I took many photos of the sky, because it changed all day: heavy clouds in the afternoon, then the sun low over the water in the evening.

One picture shows my feet in the hammock.

The orange fruit

I bought some small orange fruits during the trip. I do not remember where we stopped or what I paid. At first I ate them as they were, because I did not know they have to be boiled in salted water. Someone came over and tried to explain that you do not eat them like that. I do not remember what we did with them afterwards, and I show them below.

I am almost sure the fruit in the two photos is the same fruit. My own identification, from the photos, is pupunha, the peach palm: a palm fruit. I did not confirm it, and nobody told me the name on the boat.

Pupunha (checked November 2026)

What I found: a page on peach palm, published on April 19, 2014 and last updated on October 8, 2026, lists the fruit as caustic in its natural state and says it should be boiled before the pulp is eaten. It gives boiling in salted water for about 1 to 3 hours, and describes the fruit as yellow, orange, scarlet or brownish, and purplish when fully ripe (growables.org).

What I did not find: a source that says the fruit I bought is pupunha, because I cannot prove that from a photo. That is my guess. I will not say anything about what happens if you eat it raw, except that the page says not to.

The night of December 25: Manaus

I reached Manaus in the night between December 25 and 26. The last photo I have of the boat is of M. Fernandes at the dock, at about 01:36: the file name says 00:36, and Google Photos is one hour ahead. I do not know what time we docked, and I do not know which dock it was.

I took a taxi to Hostel Manaus, and people from the boat helped me find a taxi at that hour. I do not remember what it cost. My advice is to use an app car instead. I went straight to sleep.

Getting around Manaus by app (checked November 2026)

What I found: the Uber page for Manaus, with a 2026 copyright and no date of its own, says the app works in the city around the clock, and also offers taxis and motorbike taxis in the app (uber.com).

What I did not find: a fare for a ride from the river port to the centre, or a source on how safe it is to look for a taxi at night. I only know what happened to me.

December 26 to 30: Manaus

I stayed several days. I do not remember how many nights I spent in the hostel. I ate a lot of açaí. One idea in Brazil got to me completely: the buffet by weight. You take a plate, you load it with what you choose, they weigh it, and you pay by the weight.

On December 26, at about 18:25, I photographed a very large Christmas tree with snowmen at its foot in a square in the centre of Manaus. Google Photos places the picture in the Centro neighbourhood. I do not know the name of the square.

On December 27 I went to a beach on the river. The water is dark, almost like tea, and people were swimming. A very long cable-stayed bridge crosses the river on the horizon. I did not write down the name of the beach or the bridge, so I will not name them.

On December 29, on a city bus, two street musicians got on and played: a man with a guitar and a woman with a pan flute. I filmed about 30 seconds and show the first 20. I blurred the faces of the two musicians and left the sound as it was. I do not know where the bus was going.

On December 30 I photographed the river shore, where boats are tied up, and a park with a green lake. I do not remember where either was.

The buffet by weight (checked November 2026)

What I found: Agência Brasil reported on April 6, 2026 that the São Paulo consumer agency Procon-SP measured an average of 86.86 reais per kilo in per-kilo self-service restaurants in São Paulo in February 2026, from 350 places, and that the average ranged from about 79.49 in the north of the city to 94.36 in the west (agenciabrasil.ebc.com.br).

What I did not find: a figure for Manaus. São Paulo is much more expensive than the Amazon in most things, so I would not use that number for Manaus. I also did not find what I paid in 2017. The Portuguese name for the system is comida por quilo.

What I do not remember

I do not remember the name of the hostel in Leticia, the price of the ticket, the price of the hammock, the name of the boat company, or whether I bought a cabin or a hammock place (I slept in a hammock). I do not remember where the boat stopped on the way, what I paid for the fruit, or what else the canteen sold and for how much. I did not note the dock in Manaus, the price of the taxi, whether Hostel Manaus was the exact name of the hostel, or how many nights I slept there. I do not know where I ate the buffet or the açaí, which square the Christmas tree was in, which beach or lake I visited, or where the bus was going. I do not remember getting an exit stamp in Leticia or an entry stamp in Tabatinga, so check the border steps for your own passport. When you plan this, check the prices and the schedule at the source.
