South America · Chile · Torres del Paine

Torres del Paine, October 2017

October 25 to 27, 2017Guy Haik

Torres del Paine is a national park in Chilean Patagonia, and most travellers reach it from Puerto Natales, where I had stayed before. I spent three days in the park on foot, from Wednesday, October 25 to Friday, October 27, 2017. I slept in a tent on the first night and in a bed in a hostel on the second. I walked in and out, with a shuttle to the starting point and a bus back to Puerto Natales at the end. It rained on every day except the first.

I will say my conclusion now, so you can decide if this chapter is for you: in hindsight I would not do Torres del Paine again. It is mostly for people who love trekking. The views are beautiful, but three days is a lot of walking for someone who comes for the scenery and not for the walking itself. And on the first day I got lost on the way back from the glacier, which I explain below.

The times below are my phone's clock, and Google Photos stores them in Chilean time (GMT-3). Boxes like the next ones hold research I did in November 2026, each with a source and a date, so you can tell it apart from what I remember. The places are my reading of the photos and of the location Google Photos gives them, and I say so where I am not sure.

Entrance tickets (checked November 2026)

New system: Chile's park agency, CONAF, announced that from May 1, 2026 the old fee that depended on staying fewer or more than three days is replaced by two tickets: a single-day entry, and a multi-day entry for 2 or more days, valid for a continuous period of up to 10 days (CNN Chile, around April 29, 2026). A June 10, 2026 article says tickets are sold only online and in advance, through the official site pasesparques.cl (El Mostrador).

Price: I could not find the price list for the new system. The last foreigner prices I could verify are from before it: 34,000 Chilean pesos for an adult staying under 3 days and 48,500 for a longer stay, valid for visits until April 30, 2026 (torresdelpaine.com). That page also says tickets cannot be bought at the park gates, dates cannot be changed after purchase, and a refund is given only if CONAF closes the whole park. Check pasesparques.cl for the current price.

I do not remember what I paid in 2017 or how I bought the ticket.

Park rules and the guide requirement (checked November 2026)

Rules: a travel operator's summary of the park regulations, last edited December 15, 2025, says you may camp only in authorized, designated areas, that fires and stoves are banned outside marked areas because of wildfire risk, and that swimming in the rivers, lagoons and lakes is prohibited because of drowning risk (Vertice, park regulations). The operator sends readers to the park's own site for the official text, which I could not open.

Guides: the CNN Chile article above also describes a winter rule: anyone doing winter tourist activities on the W circuit or the Base Torres trail must have a guide authorized by CONAF, in force from April 27, 2026. My trip was in late October, so this rule would not have applied to me, but it shows how seriously the park treats safety on these trails. I did not find out what applies in spring and summer.

What to know today, in short (checked November 2026)
  • Tickets: from May 1, 2026 there are two tickets, single-day and multi-day (2 or more days, valid up to 10 days). They are sold only online and in advance, through pasesparques.cl, not at the gates. I could not find the price list for the new system, so check the site.
  • Park rules: one operator's summary says camping only in designated areas, no fires or stoves outside marked areas, and no swimming in rivers, lagoons or lakes. I could not open the park's own text to confirm.
  • Guides: since April 27, 2026 a CONAF-authorized guide is required for winter activities on the W circuit and the Base Torres trail. I did not find what applies in spring and summer.
  • My conclusion: do not walk it alone if you can avoid it.

Getting into the park

I left Puerto Natales early on Wednesday, October 25. I do not remember the company, the price, or the time of the shuttle that took me to the point where I started walking. My first photo is from 10:11, and Google Photos pins it near Estancia Pudeto, a spot on the shore of Lago Pehoé where boats cross to the other side. A white boat is moored near the shore in the photo. I do not remember if I crossed by boat, so I cannot tell you the price or the schedule.

October 25: Paine Grande and the walk to the glacier

At 11:43 Google Photos pins me at Refugio and Camping Vertice Paine Grande, one of the campsites with a shelter on the main trail. From there the trail heads toward Lago Grey and Grey Glacier. I started early and walked the whole day.

At 13:19 I passed a wooden sign that reads Laguna Los Patos, and took several photos of that lagoon and the mountains behind it.

At 14:23 I got my first view of Lago Grey, a pale grey-blue lake with chunks of ice floating at the far end. The trail kept going along the shore above the water.

Grey Glacier

Between 16:58 and 17:15 I reached the viewpoints over the front of Grey Glacier, where the ice meets the lake. There were blue icebergs in the water below.

At 17:15 I took a photo with a traveler I had met in Ushuaia. We met again there by chance. I blurred his face and I am not naming him.

Getting lost on the way back

I left early that morning. On the way back from the glacier I got lost. I do not remember where on the trail, or for how long, and I will not guess. I was lucky: I found my way again and got back before it got dark. Photos from 20:33 to 20:39 show the last light on a lake with small rocky islets, and Google Photos marks their location only as an estimate inside the park, so I cannot place them more exactly.

My conclusion

Do not walk this alone, if you can avoid it. Go with a guide, or at least in a group. Trails in a place like this can split, the weather can change, and nobody may be close enough to hear you. I was lucky. I would not count on it.

I slept in a tent that night. At each main point on the trail there is a campsite with places for tents, showers and toilets. Some people had brought their own tent. I do not remember which campsite I used, or whether I rented my tent there or carried it.

October 26: along Lago Nordenskjöld

Thursday was rainy, with heavy cloud and wind. The photos from 12:36 to 13:33 follow the shore of a long turquoise lake. I think this is Lago Nordenskjöld, from the shape of the shore and the mountains, but I have not verified it. At 13:15 there is a view of a jagged peak with snow that looks to me like Los Cuernos, "the horns", a famous peak in the park.

The fire of December 2011 (checked November 2026)

Many of the bushes and trunks in these photos are bare and grey. In December 2011, about six years before my trip, a large fire burned in the park. Al Jazeera reported on December 31, 2011 that about 11,000 hectares of woodland and scrub, nearly 4% of the park, had burned, that about 700 people, mostly tourists, were evacuated, and that the president said the park would stay closed through January (Al Jazeera). I did not check where the fire burned. I do not know whether the bare trees I photographed are from that fire.

I do not know exactly which trail I took that afternoon, and I do not know whether I went up the French Valley. The evening photos from 18:12 to 19:59 look over a turquoise lake from among trees with red flowers. Google Photos pins the 19:59 photo next to Refugio y Camping Francés, so I think this is where I slept that night. This was the night I was given a bed in a hostel room, which was lucky after the rain. I do not remember the name or the price.

October 27: the way out

On Friday, October 27 I walked out of the park. The photos start at 10:43 and run until 18:21, and the Google Photos app has no location for them, so I only have my own reading of the landscape. In the morning I followed the shore of what looks to me like the same lake. The water was very still, and the hills and snow-covered peaks were reflected in it.

After noon the trail climbed above the lake, through dark forest with an open view. The sky started to open, and for a while there was some blue.

From about 15:48 the landscape changes. I am no longer next to the long lake. The photos show a wide open valley with a braided river and a smaller pale-blue lake, snow-streaked mountains behind, and a muddy path with orange trail markers. I do not know the name of the valley or of the small lake. I assume I was heading toward where the bus back to Puerto Natales would leave, but I do not remember the place or its name.

At 16:48 and 16:50 I stopped to watch some animals on a steep slope of grey gravel. They look like small deer with antlers, and there seem to be at least three of them. I do not know their species. It might be the huemul, an endangered deer that lives in this region, or something else. I did not check.

My last two photos are from 18:19 and 18:21, in grey fog and light rain, looking over the same valley and the river. After that I got a bus back to Puerto Natales. I do not remember where I boarded, the time, or the price.

What I think now

Torres del Paine has landscapes I have not seen anywhere else, and I am glad I went. But I would not go again. It is mostly a trekking destination: the walks are long, the weather turns quickly, it rained on two of my three days, and the best sights are far from where you sleep. If you love long walks and camping, you will probably like it much more than I did. If you come mainly for the views, a day trip from Puerto Natales with a guide, or a short walk, may be enough. And whatever you choose: do not hike alone, and do not underestimate the weather.
