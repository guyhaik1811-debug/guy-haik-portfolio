People often talk about being "securely attached" or "avoidant" in relationships. The terms come from developmental research on infants, and the research is more specific and more limited than the popular version.

This is an overview from my studies, rewritten from the linked sources. It is not advice about parenting or about any specific child.

## Where it started

Attachment research has its basis in the work of John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth. In the 1950s, [a research group led by Bowlby studied children separated from their caregivers by hospitalization or foster care, and found that on return home some were over-dependent and ambivalent, while others were rejecting and avoidant](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440211047577).

## The Strange Situation

Ainsworth built on that work to create a laboratory test. In the Strange Situation, [infants face two brief separations and reunions with their familiar caregiver](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/demystifying-attachment). What gets classified is mostly how the infant behaves when the caregiver comes back.

The standard categories are [secure, insecure-avoidant and insecure-resistant (ambivalent), and a fourth, disorganized, was added later by Main and Solomon](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mary-ainsworth.html).

Ainsworth identified secure attachment as [behavior suggesting the infant's confidence in the caregiver's availability when alarmed](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/demystifying-attachment). Her student Mary Main proposed that avoidant behavior is [a conditional strategy that permits whatever closeness is possible when the caregiver tends to reject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_situation). That reading is useful because it describes avoidance as a strategy and not as a flaw.

## What the research found

A 2023 meta-analysis of more than 20,000 Strange Situation sessions [found secure attachment to be the most common pattern worldwide, with rates varying by socioeconomic risk and culture](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mary-ainsworth.html).

Attachment research has also been extended to adults. Hazan and Shaver (1987) [found a correlation between adult romantic attachment style and childhood attachment type](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=11737), although a correlation does not show that one causes the other.

## The limits

- **Culture.** One common criticism is that [the procedure was designed in the US, where independence is valued, so it may not measure the same thing everywhere](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=11737).
- **Over-reading.** Already in 1968 Ainsworth wrote to Bowlby with concern that [attachment had become a bandwagon](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/demystifying-attachment). She worried that people would expect the infant's behavior in this one test to predict too much.
- **One instrument.** Some researchers note that [the Strange Situation has long provided both the methodology and the frame of reference for the theory](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440211047577), which makes it hard to test the theory from outside.
- **Ethics.** The procedure deliberately [puts infants in a stressful situation](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=11737).

## What I take from it

The idea I find most useful is the "secure base": a child who trusts that a caregiver is available can explore more freely. But a label from a test of one-year-olds is not a verdict on a person. When someone online says "my attachment style is avoidant," I read it as a metaphor, not as a measurement.

I did not read Bowlby's or Ainsworth's original books, and I did not read the 2023 meta-analysis itself. I relied on the summaries linked above.
