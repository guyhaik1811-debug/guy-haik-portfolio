Inca Jungle to Machu Picchu: what I remember, what I don't, and what to check today

November 2026Guy Haik

In October 2017, after Cusco, I went to Machu Picchu by the Inca Jungle route instead of taking a bus or a train. It was a mix of downhill cycling, rafting, a zipline course and walking. That was nine years ago, so this is not a review of an agency. It is what I remember, what my photos show, and what I checked again in November 2026, so you can decide whether the route suits you.

What I did

On the first day I rode down a paved road in a green valley, in a helmet and body armour. The valley was steep and wide, and the road wound far below us. Later that day I went rafting, in a raft jacket and life vest, near Santa María.

On the second day I did a zipline course, with a harness, a helmet and a rope bridge over a slope. In the afternoon I was beside a river and then in a rocky gorge, and in the evening I reached Aguas Calientes.

What I do not remember

The agency. The price. How many days the tour was. Where I slept. The name of the zipline place. What I ate. I would rather leave these out than guess, and it is also why I will not tell you whether the route is good value. If a guide gives you the price of the tour I took, they did not get it from me.

How the route works today (checked November 2026)

One operator's itinerary

One operator's 4 day, 3 night itinerary (Marvelous Peru, published February 25, 2026): day 1 is Cusco to Abra Málaga, a downhill bike ride and a night in Santa María. Day 2 is a walk to Santa Teresa, with optional rafting. Day 3 is an optional zipline, the hydroelectric plant and the walk along the railway to Aguas Calientes. Day 4 is Machu Picchu and the return. The order and the optional parts depend on the operator and the weather, and rafting only runs when the river is safe.

The same operator describes the ride as starting above 4,000 m and ending at about 1,200 to 1,500 m, roughly 50 to 60 km and 3 to 4 hours with stops, mostly a continuous descent on paved road, with bikes, helmets and protective gear provided (Marvelous Peru, published March 4, 2026). It rates the risk as low if you do not ride too fast. That is an operator's own page and not an independent safety assessment, so check recent reviews of the specific agency.

My own days did not match that itinerary exactly. I rafted on the same day as the ride.

The last walk to Aguas Calientes

Checked November 2026

On the standard route, the last stretch is a walk from the Hidroeléctrica station along the railway and the Urubamba River. One guide gives it as about 10 to 11 km, 2.5 to 3.5 hours at a moderate pace, easy to moderate, with no ticket needed. It advises starting before 15:00 to avoid walking in the dark, staying on the side of the track, listening for trains and not wearing headphones (Marvelous Peru, published March 2, 2026). Another guide adds that kiosks and ATMs are limited, so carry soles, water and snacks (Scratch Your Mapa, updated October 27, 2025).

I did not find a source that says whether walking on the railway is officially permitted today, and I could not confirm that the route is open right now. Ask your agency before you go.

What I would check before booking

This is my advice from reading about the route, not something I learned on it.

  • The real itinerary. Ask how many hours are cycling, rafting, zipline and walking. Some tours list four activities and give one or two of them very little time.
  • What is included. Bikes, helmets and protective gear, rafting and zipline (these are sometimes optional and cost extra), meals, beds, transport to the start, and the Machu Picchu ticket. Do not assume the ticket is included.
  • Safety. Ask who provides the helmets and armour, how the group rides, and what happens if the river is too high for rafting.
  • Group size and sleeping arrangements. Ask how many people and whether rooms are private or shared.
  • Recent reviews of the agency. Read ones that talk about the guides, the bikes and the safety, not only the star rating.

Is it worth it?

I cannot answer this on price, because I do not remember what I paid. What I can say is that you are choosing a few days of activities over a direct trip. If you only want to see Machu Picchu, the train is simpler. If you like being outdoors and moving, and you want the way there to be part of the trip, this route gives you that.

What I remember most is not an activity. It is the evening in Aguas Calientes, when Peru played Argentina in a World Cup qualifier and the whole square watched it on a screen. I did not plan that evening, and I did not read about it in any guide.
